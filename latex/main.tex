While Preserving Next-Token Prediction}

\begin{abstract}
Next-token prediction rewards one observed continuation even when several words
would express the same meaning in context. We study how to reward acceptable
lexical alternatives without sacrificing the model's probability for the
observed text. We reconstruct the printed objective and the code-form objective
from Iyer et al. (2026), extend candidate scoring to complete token sequences,
and compare them with data augmentation and a retention-aware configuration on
SWORDS. On a held-out development split with Llama-3.2-1B, our configuration
outperforms data augmentation in every one of three seeds on substitute ranking,
acceptable-alternative likelihood, observed-target likelihood, and ordinary
language-model likelihood. The strongest unconstrained concept objective ranks
substitutes better and incurs a larger retention cost. This comparison defines
the ranking-retention tradeoff that concept-aware training should report.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A language model learns from the word that happened to occur in a sentence. If
the sentence contains \emph{chaos}, standard next-token prediction rewards
\emph{chaos}; it gives no direct credit to contextually acceptable alternatives
such as \emph{turmoil} or \emph{mayhem}. This training signal is precise, but it
does not represent the range of valid lexical choices available to a speaker.

Iyer et al. (2026) propose next-concept prediction (NCP), which supplies a set of
synonyms or hypernyms at selected positions \citep{iyer2026beyond}. Their work
raises a practical question: how much probability can be assigned to valid
alternatives before the model becomes worse at the text it actually observes?
Answering this question requires a common evaluation protocol. A concept loss
and ordinary next-token loss have different scales, so their raw training losses
cannot be compared as perplexities. Candidate strings may also contain several
subword tokens, which requires sequence-level scoring.

We give a controlled study of this tradeoff. We use SWORDS, where substitutes
are judged by humans in context \citep{lee2021swords}, and train every system from
the same Llama-3.2-1B checkpoint. Our main contribution is a retention-aware NCP
configuration that combines ordinary language-model training, exact
sequence-level concept supervision, and replay of original sentences. On the
development split, it improves every main point estimate over the paper's data
augmentation baseline while keeping both the observed-target loss and ordinary
language-model loss lower.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We separate three quantities that earlier comparisons mixed: the
    likelihood of ordinary held-out text, the likelihood of the observed target
    at a concept slot, and the likelihood of other human-accepted alternatives.
    \item We provide differentiable, full-sequence implementations of the
    printed NCP equation and the objective suggested by the released trainer,
    and describe the relation between them without assuming which code version
    produced the published results.
    \item We show across three seeds that concept supervision has a tunable
    ranking-retention frontier. Our balanced setting improves over data
    augmentation on all main development metrics, while a less constrained
    concept objective gives the best ranking at a larger retention cost.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Concept-aware language modelling.}
Iyer et al. (2026) compare ordinary next-token training with concept-aware data
augmentation and a custom objective over synonym and hypernym sets
\citep{iyer2026beyond}. We use their central idea and focus on a narrower issue:
the balance between alternative-word probability and retention of ordinary
language modelling. Our study uses a smaller model and human-labelled contextual
substitutes, so it is a controlled extension rather than a numerical replication
of their 8B experiments.

\paragraph{Lexical substitution and lexical entailment.}
SWORDS evaluates whether a candidate can replace a target word in its specific
sentence and provides graded human labels \citep{lee2021swords}. This makes it a
direct test of synonym-oriented NCP. HyperLex instead measures the graded,
directional relation ``is $x$ a type of $y$?'' without sentence context
\citep{vulic2017hyperlex}. We use HyperLex and bm-semlex
\citep{ivan2024bmsemlex} as diagnostics. They do not replace SWORDS as the
primary benchmark because the present models are trained on contextual
substitution rather than directional hypernymy.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Task and notation}

Each training example contains a left context $x$, an observed target $y$, and a
set $A(x)$ of other substitutes that SWORDS annotators accept in that context.
The observed target is the word already present in the human-authored sentence.
For example, if $y$ is \emph{chaos}, $A(x)$ may contain \emph{turmoil} and
\emph{mayhem}. We use $C(x)$ for the set supplied to the concept loss. Depending
on the experiment, $C(x)$ is either $A(x)$ or $A(x)\cup\{y\}$.

Table~\ref{tab:notation} defines the terms used in the objectives. Negative
log-likelihood (NLL) is the negative logarithm of probability. Lower NLL means
that the model assigns more probability to the evaluated continuation.

Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$x$ & tokens to the left of the target \\
$y$ & target observed in the sentence \\
$A(x)$ & human-accepted alternatives to $y$ \\
$C(x)$ & candidates used by the concept loss \\
$c=(c_1,\ldots,c_m)$ & one candidate after tokenisation \\
$w$ & weight on ordinary language modelling \\
$\alpha$ & weight on concept supervision \\
\bottomrule

\subsection{Full-sequence candidate probability}

A lexical substitute may contain one token or several subword tokens. We score
the complete sequence with teacher forcing:
\begin{equation}
\log p(c\mid x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log p(c_j\mid x,c_{<j}).
\label{eq:sequence}
\end{equation}
The context and candidate are tokenised together, preserving the same leading
space and word-boundary convention during training and evaluation. Context is
left-truncated when necessary; candidate tokens are never truncated. Exact
sequence probability from Equation~\ref{eq:sequence} is used for losses and
probability mass. SWORDS ranking uses mean log-probability per candidate token,
which prevents tokenisation length alone from deciding the ranking.

\subsection{Training losses}

\paragraph{Ordinary language modelling.}
The causal language-model loss, $L_{\mathrm{CLM}}$, predicts every observed token
in each training sequence. At the target position its contribution is
$-\log p(y\mid x)$. This term preserves the model's ability to predict the text
that was actually written.

\paragraph{Per-candidate concept loss.}
The first concept objective averages the sequence NLL over the candidate set:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{pc}}(x)=-\frac{1}{|C(x)|}\sum_{c\in C(x)}\log p(c\mid x).
\label{eq:percandidate}
\end{equation}
The factor $1/|C(x)|$ prevents examples with more candidates from receiving a
larger loss merely because their sets are larger. Every candidate contributes
equally, so this loss directly raises weak alternatives as well as strong ones.

\paragraph{Set-marginal loss.}
We also test a loss that rewards total probability assigned to the set:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{set}}(x)=-\log\sum_{c\in C(x)}p(c\mid x).
\label{eq:set}
\end{equation}
This objective can improve by concentrating on whichever member is already
probable. It therefore asks whether the model knows some acceptable form,
whereas Equation~\ref{eq:percandidate} asks it to raise each form. The
development ablation in Appendix~\ref{sec:ablations} favours the per-candidate
loss, so the main method uses Equation~\ref{eq:percandidate}.

\paragraph{Combined objective.}
All concept systems are instances of
\begin{equation}
L=wL_{\mathrm{CLM}}+\alpha L_{\mathrm{concept}}.
\label{eq:combined}
\end{equation}
Here $w$ controls retention and $\alpha$ controls how strongly the model learns
the alternatives. Increasing $\alpha$ should improve concept metrics until the
extra pressure begins to reduce observed-target or ordinary-text likelihood.

\subsection{Relation to Iyer et al. (2026)}

The source paper presents
$L(T^*\mid S)=|T^*|^{-1}\sum_n\log p(t_n\in T^*\mid S,\Theta)$ as an objective to
maximise \citep{iyer2026beyond}. After changing maximisation to minimisation,
this corresponds to Equation~\ref{eq:percandidate} with $w=0$ and $\alpha=1$.
We call this interpretation \peq{}.

The released trainer also starts from the ordinary language-model loss and then
subtracts the mean candidate log-probability. Its mathematical form therefore
corresponds to $w=1$ and $\alpha=1$ in Equation~\ref{eq:combined}. We call our
differentiable reconstruction of that form \pcode{}. The candidate probabilities
in the released file we inspected are evaluated inside
\texttt{torch.no\_grad()}, so that candidate term does not contribute to the
parameter gradient in that version. For a useful comparison, \peq{} and
\pcode{} both compute full-sequence candidate probabilities inside the gradient
graph. This treatment separates the printed formulation, the form suggested by
the surrounding trainer, and the updates produced by the released file. We do
not infer which internal code version produced the published experiments.\footnote{
We inspected commit \texttt{a955e754} of
\url{https://github.com/layaiyer1/concept-aware-training}.}

\subsection{Systems compared}

The main comparison uses four training procedures and one untouched reference.

\paragraph{Untouched model (\base{}).}
Llama-3.2-1B is evaluated before post-training. It establishes whether additional
training helps relative to using the public checkpoint directly.

\paragraph{Data augmentation (\aug{}).}
Each accepted substitute replaces the target in the complete sentence, producing
ordinary language-model training examples. This is the data augmentation
baseline described in the source paper and requires no custom loss.

\paragraph{Printed concept objective (\peq{}).}
This system minimises Equation~\ref{eq:percandidate} with $w=0$, $\alpha=1$, and
$C(x)=A(x)$. It is the literal differentiable sequence-level reading of the
printed objective.

\paragraph{Code-form objective (\pcode{}).}
This system uses $L_{\mathrm{CLM}}+L_{\mathrm{pc}}$, matching the weights in the
released trainer while restoring gradients through candidate scores. Its
candidate set excludes the observed target, as in the concept data derived from
the source procedure.

\paragraph{Balanced NCP (\method{}, ours).}
Our system uses Equation~\ref{eq:combined} with $w=1$, $\alpha=4$, and the
observed target included in $C(x)$. It mixes the concept rows with replayed
original sentences. Inclusion prevents the concept term from opposing the
ordinary loss on $y$, while replay supplies ordinary next-token updates. The
weight $\alpha=4$ is selected from the development sweep in
Section~\ref{sec:alpha}; the official test set has not been used.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Data}

SWORDS contains contexts, target locations, candidate substitutes, and human
acceptability judgements. We treat candidates with an acceptability score of at
least $0.5$ as acceptable and candidates at $0.0$ as rejected. We retain targets
with at least two acceptable substitutes. This produces 313 usable targets from
the 370-target SWORDS development set, with a mean of 6.62 accepted substitutes
per target. A deterministic split assigns 251 usable targets to training and 62
to validation. Evaluation follows the original 80/20 target-ID split and covers
74 held-out target IDs; targets that do not meet the training filter remain
available to ranking metrics when the benchmark provides candidates.

Data augmentation creates 1,607 training sentences. We use 1,607 rows for every
trained system: original sentences are repeated for ordinary controls, concept
rows are cycled for the source-style objectives, and \method{} combines 251
concept rows with 1,356 replay rows. This matches the number of rows and optimiser
steps. It does not exactly match the number of tokens because lexical
substitutions can change sentence length.

\subsection{Model and optimisation}

All systems start from Llama-3.2-1B \citep{grattafiori2024llama}. We train for two
epochs with AdamW, learning rate $10^{-5}$, cosine decay, 10\% warmup, bfloat16,
sequence length 128, effective batch size 16, and candidate microbatches of eight.
We use three random seeds: 42, 7, and 123. Candidate supervision is stored by row
identifier rather than context text, which preserves duplicate contexts and
makes cached and uncached preprocessing identical. Unit tests verify exact
single-token and multi-token scoring, padding masks, candidate gradients, and
training-evaluation tokenisation agreement.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

We report five complementary quantities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GAP} is SWORDS generalised average precision. It compares the
    model's complete candidate ranking with graded human acceptability. Higher is
    better.
    \item \textbf{AUROC} measures how well the score separates accepted from
    rejected substitutes across decision thresholds. A value of 0.5 is random;
    higher is better.
    \item \textbf{Alternative NLL} averages $-\log p(c\mid x)$ over accepted
    substitutes other than the observed target. It directly measures whether
    concept training raises the alternatives. Lower is better.
    \item \textbf{Observed-target NLL} is $-\log p(y\mid x)$ at the target slot.
    It measures the local retention cost of concept training. Lower is better.
    \item \textbf{Ordinary NLL} is the mean next-token NLL on held-out original
    YouTube text. Perplexity is $\exp(\text{ordinary NLL})$. These metrics test
    whether post-training preserves general next-token prediction; lower is better.
\end{itemize}
GAP is the primary task metric. Alternative NLL explains whether a ranking gain
comes with more probability on accepted words. Observed-target and ordinary NLL
measure two retention costs: one at the supervised lexical slot and one over
every token in a separate held-out corpus. We report mean and sample standard
deviation over seeds. Planned comparisons use paired bootstrap intervals over
the same target IDs with 5,000 resamples. The selected $\alpha=4$ comparison
still requires its paired interval before confirmatory reporting.

\subsection{Concept quality and retention form a frontier}

\peq{} and \pcode{} give the strongest rankings. Their GAP scores are 0.4559 and
0.4553, compared with 0.4270 for \method{}. The retention measurements change the
interpretation. \peq{} raises observed-target NLL from 4.446 to 6.159 and ordinary
NLL from 2.060 to 2.283. Adding the language-model term in \pcode{} recovers much
of that loss, but its observed-target NLL remains 0.561 higher than \method{} and
its ordinary NLL remains 0.063 higher.

These systems occupy different useful points. \pcode{} is preferable when
substitute ranking is the sole objective. \method{} is preferable under the
practical constraint that concept training should preserve next-token
prediction. The current evidence supports a tradeoff rather than a single winner
across all metrics.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments clarify why earlier results appeared inconsistent. A low concept
loss alone does not imply better language modelling. \peq{} demonstrates this:
it has the lowest alternative NLL and the highest GAP, yet the worst
observed-target NLL. The ordinary loss in \pcode{} recovers much of this cost.
Replay and observed-target inclusion push further toward retention in \method{}.

The choice between per-candidate and set-marginal training also matters. The
per-candidate loss continues to reward a weak alternative, while the set marginal
can rely on a single high-probability member. SWORDS evaluates a ranking over many
human-accepted substitutes, so the per-candidate signal fits the benchmark more
closely. The contrastive term adds little because the model already separates
accepted from rejected candidates reasonably well before training. Detailed
ablations are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:ablations}.

\section{Conclusion}

Concept-aware post-training works at the 1B scale: it raises the likelihood and
ranking of human-accepted substitutes across three seeds. Its benefit depends on
how retention is handled. The code-form reconstruction produces the strongest
SWORDS ranking, while our balanced configuration improves every main development
point estimate over data augmentation and preserves next-token prediction more
effectively. The immediate next experiment is fixed in advance: rerun the paired
intervals for the selected configuration, lock $\alpha=4$, train on the complete
SWORDS development set, and evaluate the official test set once.

\section*{Limitations}

The main table reports a development split. We selected $\alpha$ on that split,
so the \method{} row is optimistic until confirmed on the untouched SWORDS test
set. The held-out split contains 74 target IDs, which limits the resolution of
ranking metrics, and the paired bootstrap comparison for $\alpha=4$ has not yet
been run. We match rows and optimiser steps rather than exact token counts. All
trained systems use one 1B model family; the source paper used a Llama-3-8B model,
so scale and architecture remain open. SWORDS thresholds turn graded judgements
into accepted and rejected sets, and other thresholds may change the balance.
Our comments on the released trainer apply to the public commit cited above and
do not establish which private version produced earlier experiments.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work uses public lexical benchmarks and does not collect personal data.
Concept sets can encode annotator and lexical-resource biases, and raising the
probability of all members may reproduce those biases. Human acceptability is
context-dependent, so these objectives should not be treated as universal rules
about meaning.

\section{Additional metric}

Precision at one is the fraction of targets for which the model's highest-ranked
candidate is accepted. It is easy to interpret, but on 74 targets it changes in
steps of $1/74$. The mean values are 0.243 for \base{}, 0.306 for \aug{}, 0.432
for \peq{}, 0.437 for \pcode{}, and 0.324 for \method{}. We therefore retain GAP
as the primary ranking metric.
